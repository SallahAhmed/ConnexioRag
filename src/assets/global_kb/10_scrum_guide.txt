Scrum is an agile framework for managing complex work that provides a structured yet flexible approach to product development. It was formalized in the Scrum Guide by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland and is the most widely adopted agile methodology. Scrum is built on the premise that work should be organized into fixed-length iterations called sprints, typically lasting between one and four weeks, with two weeks being the most common duration for software development teams. Each sprint delivers a potentially releasable product increment.

Scrum defines three key roles with specific responsibilities. The Product Owner is responsible for maximizing the value of the product resulting from the work of the Scrum Team. They manage the Product Backlog, clearly express backlog items, order them to best achieve goals, and ensure the Development Team understands them. The Scrum Master is a servant-leader who facilitates Scrum events, coaches the team in Scrum practices, removes impediments that block progress, and helps the organization understand and adopt Scrum. The Development Team is a self-organizing, cross-functional group of professionals who do the work of delivering a potentially releasable Increment of Done product at the end of each Sprint. Development Teams are typically three to nine people.

Scrum includes five formal events for inspection and adaptation. Sprint Planning kicks off each sprint by defining what can be delivered and how. Daily Scrum is a 15-minute time-boxed event for the Development Team to synchronize activities and create a plan for the next 24 hours. Sprint Review is held at the end of the sprint to inspect the Increment and adapt the Product Backlog. Sprint Retrospective concludes the sprint by examining the team's processes and creating a plan for improvements. The Sprint itself is a container event for all other events. Key artifacts include the Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, and the Increment, each with defined commitments of the Product Goal, Sprint Goal, and Definition of Done.
