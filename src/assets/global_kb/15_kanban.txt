Kanban is an agile methodology that focuses on visualizing work, limiting work in progress (WIP) is, and maximizing the flow of value through the system. Kanban originated from the Toyota Production System and was adapted for knowledge work and software development. Unlike Scrum which uses fixed-length iterations, Kanban is a continuous flow system where work items move through the workflow as capacity becomes available.

The Kanban board is the central visual management tool. Work items are represented on cards that move through columns representing workflow stages such as To Do, In Progress, In Review, Testing, and Done. Each column has a WIP limit that caps the maximum number of work items allowed in that stage simultaneously. WIP limits are the key mechanism for identifying bottlenecks. When a column hits its limit, the team must focus on completing work in that stage before pulling new work in. This prevents multitasking and ensures that work actually finishes rather than having many items partially done.

Kanban is built on four core principles. Visualize the workflow by making all work items and their status visible to everyone. Limit WIP by setting explicit caps at each workflow stage to prevent overloading the team and to expose bottlenecks. Manage flow by measuring and optimizing cycle time, which is the time from when work starts to when it finishes, and lead time, which is the time from when work is requested to when it finishes. Make process policies explicit so everyone understands how work moves through the system. Implement feedback loops through regular service delivery reviews and operations reviews. Kanban is particularly well-suited for maintenance teams, support teams, and any environment with unpredictable or continuous work inflow. Many teams combine Scrum for planning with Kanban for visualization by using a Scrum board with Kanban-style WIP limits.
